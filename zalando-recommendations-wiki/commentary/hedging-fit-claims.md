# Hedging fit claims

**evidence: sourced**

## The rule

Fit-confidence language must be a direct function of real evidence — never asserted flat. Production fit-confidence systems (e.g. True Fit) score a fit prediction on: number of data points, recency of those data points, whether sizing was extrapolated from other brands/products rather than measured, and measured sizing consistency within the brand.

Source: https://www.truefit.com/how-it-works

Verbal hedges calibrated to actual confidence work better than fake numeric precision — a percentage implies more certainty than the underlying data supports; graded, honest phrasing keeps trust calibrated to reality.

Source: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3816046.3816231

## The phrasing table

| Evidence available | Approved phrasing |
|---|---|
| High volume, recent, same-brand fit reports | "Runs true to size." |
| Some signal, but thin or mixed | "Fit reports are mixed — consider sizing up." |
| Extrapolated from other brands/products, or a new item | "We don't have enough fit data on this one yet." |
| None | Say nothing about fit. Don't fill the silence with a generic size-chart link and call it equivalent. |

## Why this is a hard rule, not a style preference

A false fit claim is worse than no claim — a mediocre recommendation costs little, an unsupported fit assertion that turns out wrong costs a return, a bad experience, and calibrated trust in every future claim this system makes. This is the single highest-stakes rule in the whole wiki, because size/fit issues already drive 50-67% of fashion e-commerce returns even without the system making false claims on top of that — see [../needs/size-fit-anxiety.md](../needs/size-fit-anxiety.md).
