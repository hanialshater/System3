# Appendix: The Ideas Behind System 3

*A summary of philosophy of science and the other traditions used in this book*

I did not invent the ideas this book runs on. Most of them are older than I am, and several are older than the computer. Philosophy of science supplies the account of evidence, criticism and inquiry. Philosophy of language helps say what a fluent model can and cannot establish. Architecture, knowledge engineering and cybernetics supply ways to make knowledge usable. Institutional theory and the study of human choice carry the argument into power and purpose.

The first part follows the debates surveyed in Peter Godfrey-Smith’s *Theory and Reality* (2003), the book I would hand to anyone who wants the long version. For each position it gives the idea, the difficulty it ran into, and where it turns up in these chapters. The second part names the other sources. The afterword says what this book proposes to add.[1](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-godfrey-smith)

## I. Philosophy of Science: The Main Ideas

### Logical Positivism and Logical Empiricism: Moritz Schlick, Rudolf Carnap and the Vienna Circle

The Vienna Circle wanted to combine empiricism with logical analysis. Scientific claims should be connected to experience, and logic should make their meaning and relations clear. Its early verificationism tried to separate meaningful factual claims from metaphysical ones by asking whether anything could verify them.

The trouble is that no finite set of observations conclusively verifies a universal law, observation language is not a neutral foundation, and theoretical statements do not reduce one by one to observations. Later logical empiricists developed more flexible accounts of confirmation. The failure did not make evidence irrelevant. It ended the hope that one logical reconstruction could settle meaning, justification and scientific practice together.

*In this book.* The hope is Leibniz’s *Calculemus* from the preface. The lesson is why System 3 is an institution and not a calculus.

### Induction and Confirmation: David Hume, Carl Hempel and Nelson Goodman

How does what we have observed justify a conclusion about what we have not? Hume’s problem is that the expectation that the future will resemble the past cannot be justified deductively, and defending it by its past success already uses induction.

Hempel’s ravens expose difficulties in simple accounts of confirming a generalization. Goodman’s *grue* asks why some descriptions support reasonable projections while specially constructed ones do not. Counting favorable cases is not enough. The concepts, the alternatives and the procedure through which the evidence was obtained all matter.

*In this book.* Forty faded launches on a home screen say nothing about checkout speed (Chapter 6), and a pattern earns its confidence only on cases that did not produce it.

### Falsification: Karl Popper

A scientific theory should risk failure. One that forbids some possible observations exposes itself to a test; one that can accommodate any outcome tells us little. Passing a test leaves a theory standing without proving it.

An apparent falsification also implicates the instruments and the auxiliary assumptions, and working scientists reasonably persist in the face of anomalies. Popper’s opposition to inductive confirmation also leaves it unclear why a well-tested theory deserves reliance tomorrow. System 3 takes exposure to failure from Popper without adopting the rest of his philosophy.[2](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-popper)

*In this book.* The evaluator that cannot be charmed (Chapter 2), predictions written down before the result (Chapters 6 and 11), and the line in every pattern file that says what would make it wrong.

### Confirmation Holism: Pierre Duhem and W. V. O. Quine

Evidence meets a bundle of commitments. A predicted result depends on a theory, assumptions about the situation, instruments and interpretation, and a contrary result does not say, by logic alone, which of them should change.

Duhem developed the difficulty for physical testing. Quine argued for a wider web of belief whose parts can be adjusted when experience conflicts with expectation. Their positions differ in scope. The practical consequence is the same: investigate the competing accounts of the failure before discarding the headline theory or blaming the apparatus.[3](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-duhem)

*In this book.* Boyle’s pump and the failing integration test (Chapter 5), the probes that told a logging bug from a novelty effect (Chapter 6), and claims stored with what they rest on.

### Paradigms, Normal Science and Revolutions: Thomas Kuhn

Scientific work depends on shared frameworks, exemplary solutions and standards. In normal science, investigators solve demanding problems inside that inheritance without reopening every fundamental assumption, and the commitment is productive.

Persistent anomalies can bring crisis and a change of framework, and a revolution may change the questions, concepts and standards by which explanations are compared. Kuhn complicates the picture of science as a steady pile of verified facts. He also raises hard questions about comparison and progress across frameworks, and need not be read as saying every framework is as good as another.[4](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-kuhn)

*In this book.* The next worker who inherits the whole library on arrival (Chapter 6), and the worry running through Part III that a judge trained inside a method cannot see past it.

### Research Programmes: Imre Lakatos

Appraise a developing programme, not one isolated hypothesis. A programme keeps a core while revising the assumptions around it. It is progressive when its revisions predict something new that then holds up, and degenerating when they only accommodate the difficulties already met.

This permits rational patience without treating every rescue story as progress. A promising programme may need time to build its instruments or answer an anomaly. The hard practical question is when patience stops buying discovery, and Lakatos supplies a way to judge a trajectory, not a deadline for abandoning it.[5](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-lakatos)

*In this book.* Wegener’s decades as program B (Chapter 5), and the holiday, promotion and competitor stories that each explained one fade and predicted none (Chapter 6).

### Problem Solving, Acceptance and Pursuit: Larry Laudan

Scientific progress consists in solving empirical and conceptual problems, and research traditions can be judged by the problems they solve and the difficulties they create.

Laudan separates *acceptance* from *pursuit*. The theory we have best reason to believe today need not be the only one worth investigating tomorrow. A less established alternative can deserve resources for what it might solve, and treating belief and funding as one decision can keep it from ever gathering the evidence that would make it credible.[6](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-laudan)

*In this book.* The budget kept away from the leader (Chapter 5), the small budget for evaluating a pattern (Chapter 6), and Laudan’s reticulated model behind the constitutional surface (Chapter 7).

### Methodological Pluralism: Paul Feyerabend

The history of scientific advance resists universal prescriptions of method. Rules later treated as essential were sometimes broken in productive work, and alternative theories can expose assumptions that are invisible from inside the dominant account.

Feyerabend’s “anything goes” challenges the philosopher’s authority to legislate one method for all inquiry. It does not supply a procedure. System 3 takes the narrower lesson that methods must stay open to challenge, and admits that its own requirement to test an amendment is a methodological commitment too.[7](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-feyerabend)

*In this book.* The last, unmarked pattern of Chapter 6, where the procedure decides which failures count, and creative distrust aimed at the system’s own organization (Chapter 10).

### Sociology of Science: Robert Merton, David Bloor, Steven Shapin, Simon Schaffer and Bruno Latour

Knowledge is produced by institutions, with incentives, instruments and social conditions. Merton examined scientific norms and rewards. The Strong Programme associated with Bloor sought causal explanations of beliefs judged true as well as beliefs judged false. Shapin and Schaffer studied experimental practice and authority, and Latour followed science through the work and the networks that establish a result.

These approaches disagree with one another. What they share is the charge that an account limited to logical relations between theories and facts leaves most of real science unexplained. Explaining how a belief acquired authority is still a different thing from showing that it is well supported.

*In this book.* Hobbes against the gentlemen in Boyle’s room, Newton recognized by his claw (Chapter 5), and the feathers of Chapter 6.

### Feminist Epistemology and Critical Interaction: Sandra Harding, Evelyn Fox Keller and Helen Longino

Background assumptions, exclusions and social position affect which questions get asked and how evidence is read. A varied community can notice what a homogeneous one takes for granted.

Longino gives this an institutional form. Effective criticism needs venues, uptake, shared standards and a tempered equality of intellectual authority, and objectivity is strengthened by organized interaction among differently placed knowers. Uptake means criticism can change what the community is committed to; it does not mean accepting every objection. Tempered equality gives standing to contribute without pretending all expertise is the same.[8](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-longino)

*In this book.* The nurses who knew and could not say (Chapter 5), the one-word reply *noted* (Chapter 6), and the rule that an objection travels with the decision.

### Naturalism: John Dewey, W. V. O. Quine and Peter Godfrey-Smith

Study knowledge with what we have learned about actual knowers and their surroundings. Psychology, history and the other sciences can inform epistemology, and philosophy does not first have to find an infallible standpoint outside all inquiry.

There are several versions. Quine proposed absorbing epistemology into psychology; Godfrey-Smith keeps philosophical questions about assessment and organization while drawing on empirical work. Observation can depend on concepts and instruments without losing contact with reality, and how reliable that contact is becomes one more thing to investigate.

*In this book.* Trust that grows upward from a face, the farmer and the cow (Chapter 4), and Peirce’s sense of contact with reality (Chapter 5).

### Social Epistemology and the Division of Labor: David Hull, Philip Kitcher and Kevin Zollman

What is rational for one investigator and what helps a community discover can come apart. Hull studied science as a social process of credit, competition and cooperation. Kitcher examined how effort should be divided among approaches, and showed that everyone choosing the current favorite can be a poor collective allocation.[9](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-kitcher)

Zollman’s models show that the structure of communication affects learning, including conditions where keeping investigations apart for a while prevents premature convergence. That is no universal prescription for isolation. The unit to assess is the population, with its incentives and its flow of information.[10](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-zollman)

*In this book.* Who gets the next agent (Chapter 5), critics who see the artifact before the builder’s account (Chapters 3 and 5), and the operator who quietly did everyone’s work (Chapter 11).

### Scientific Realism and Its Critics: Godfrey-Smith’s Survey

Science can aim to describe the world’s actual structure, including what we cannot observe directly. Realists treat at least some theoretical claims as more than devices for predicting observations.

The challenges are familiar: theories that worked and were later rejected, and rival theories compatible with the same evidence. Empiricist critics ask how far evidence warrants commitment to unobservable structure. A fallible realism can grant both and investigate which instruments and inferences in fact give reliable access. Successful prediction is important evidence. It does not make every part of a theory true.

*In this book.* Galileo’s moons through a doubted telescope, and Wegener right about the continents and wrong about what moved them (Chapter 5).

### Explanation: Carl Hempel, Wesley Salmon and Philip Kitcher

Prediction and explanation are different achievements. Hempel’s deductive-nomological account explains by deriving a phenomenon from laws and conditions. But a shadow lets us calculate a flagpole’s height without explaining why the pole is that tall.

Causal accounts, associated with Salmon, look for the processes that produced the phenomenon. Unification accounts, developed by Kitcher and others, explain by connecting many phenomena through fewer patterns. Either way an explanation must do more than reproduce a number, and a coherent story about why a metric moved can remain unsupported.

*In this book.* The holiday story and Bing’s rising numbers on a degraded page (Chapter 6), and Pearl’s ladder when a company reads only its own dashboards (Chapter 11).

### Bayesianism and Procedural Evidence: Frank Ramsey and Godfrey-Smith

Belief comes in degrees. Bayesian updating compares how expected the evidence would be under rival hypotheses and turns a prior into a posterior. Ramsey helped establish the connection between partial belief, consistency and choice.[11](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-ramsey)

The calculus does not choose good priors, guarantee a sound model or certify a sample. Godfrey-Smith’s *procedural naturalism* stresses how the evidence was produced and which alternatives the procedure could tell apart. A randomly sampled observation and a selectively collected one can look identical and warrant different inferences. Evidence needs an account of the encounter as well as the resulting sentence.

*In this book.* The camel’s seven claims (Chapter 4), Alexander’s asterisks and the confidence line in a pattern file (Chapter 6), and a knowledge base read as testimony, with beliefs attached (Chapter 11).

### The Synthesis: Empiricism, Naturalism and Realism

Godfrey-Smith’s closing picture combines an empirical strategy with a social organization. Ideas, ambitious theoretical ones included, are developed so that experience can constrain them. Institutions organize specialization, criticism, cooperation, competition and the keeping of results.

Knowers are organisms already interacting with the world, using instruments to extend their reach. Theories and experiments are built through those interactions, and reality constrains what survives. A community can succeed scientifically because of how it is organized, even when its members are individually biased or stubborn. This is the philosophical starting point closest to System 3’s society of fallible knowers.[1](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-godfrey-smith)

*In this book.* The reveal page, and every chapter after it.

## II. Other Sources of the Book’s Ideas

### Language, Meaning and Grounding: Saussure, Wittgenstein and Derrida

**Saussure:** signs acquire value through relationships and differences within a linguistic system. **Wittgenstein:** meaning belongs to use within activities and forms of life. **Derrida:** signs can be repeated in new circumstances; context cannot finally close their interpretation.[12](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-saussure)[13](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-wittgenstein)[14](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-derrida)

These are distinct accounts. In Chapters 4 and 12, they help separate competence inside language from warranted claims about what happens outside it. “Gradient descent is the answer to Derrida” is my provocation: productive operation within ambiguity does not establish that Derrida was refuted.

### External Knowledge and Epistemic Dependence: Popper and John Hardwig

**Popper’s World 3:** theories, arguments and problems become objects others can examine beyond their authors’ mental lives. **Hardwig’s epistemic dependence:** knowing much requires reliance on work we cannot personally reproduce.[15](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-popper-world3)[16](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-hardwig)

Chapters 5 and 6 ask what changes when artificial participants can retrieve, use and revise that inheritance. The trust chain matters because the library contains errors as well as knowledge. A single fluent voice does not make the underlying dependencies disappear.

### Patterns and Knowledge Engineering: Christopher Alexander, Jon Doyle and Edward Feigenbaum

**Alexander’s pattern language:** practical knowledge travels with context, reasons and links to other patterns. **Doyle’s truth maintenance:** program beliefs retain justifications so changes can propagate. **Feigenbaum’s knowledge engineering:** useful systems depend heavily on domain knowledge and the difficulty of acquiring it.[17](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-alexander)[18](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-doyle)[19](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-feigenbaum)

Chapter 6 combines those inheritances in patterns a worker can execute and challenge. Extraction can become cheaper while validation remains difficult. Maintaining justifications does not establish that the justifications are true.

### Learning, Search and Emergence: Sutton and the Agent Research

**Sutton’s temporal-difference learning** and the reinforcement-learning tradition show how behavior can improve through consequences rather than supplied answers. Program-search systems such as FunSearch and AlphaEvolve extend learning and search into algorithm construction.[20](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-td)[21](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-alphaevolve)

Chapters 1–3 and 7 follow control from the individual answer into the conditions of search: representations, environments, feedback and evaluation. Emergence does not mean that nobody designed the components or chose the reward.

### Cybernetics and the Limits of Oversight: W. Ross Ashby and Roger Conant

**Ashby’s requisite variety:** effective regulation requires sufficient capacity to handle the relevant disturbances. **Conant and Ashby’s good-regulator theorem:** under its specified conditions, a successful regulator requires a model of what it regulates.[22](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-regulator)

Chapter 8 uses this lineage to ask how a weaker overseer can constrain a more capable system. Monitoring, interpretability and independent checks help expose behavior. No generic instruction to supervise supplies the missing ability to understand or detect every failure.

### Commons and Institutional Diversity: Elinor Ostrom

**Central idea:** shared resources can be governed through arrangements users develop, monitor and revise. State control and private ownership do not exhaust the possibilities. Conditions and local circumstances matter; no single institutional template works everywhere.[23](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-ostrom)

Chapter 12 connects this to constructive capacity. Tools for learning from other cases and exploring proposed rules could expand what a small community can attempt. That application is my proposal. Ostrom’s work also reminds us that participants need standing in the rules, not simply better calculations supplied to their rulers.

### Human Choice, Learning and Alignment: Kahneman, Ebbinghaus, Sarasvathy and Russell

**Kahneman’s two-system framework** supplies the cognitive comparison from which the title departs. **Ebbinghaus and later learning research** examine forgetting, practice and retrieval. **Sarasvathy’s effectuation** describes goals developing through action with available means. **Russell’s beneficial-AI approach** makes uncertainty about human preferences central.[24](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-kahneman)[25](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-ebbinghaus)[26](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-sarasvathy)[27](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-russell)

Chapters 9 and 12 bring these questions together: people learn slowly, discover and revise purposes, and can become dependent on assistance. The human participates in reconsidering objectives. System 3 is an external institutional proposal, not an empirically established third psychological processing system.

### Engineering Practice and Measurement

Tests, runbooks, incident reviews, load tests, experiment diagnostics and version control provide working forms of memory and correction. Kohavi and colleagues’ work on online experiments shows how correct headline measurements can support wrong conclusions. Goodhart’s analysis, and Strathern’s familiar formulation, warn about using targeted measures as if they retained their old informational role.[28](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-kohavi)[29](appendix-references.md#ref-guide-goodhart)

These practices make the philosophical problems concrete. A passed test can miss the customer’s difficulty; a postmortem can preserve a lesson without changing a future decision. The institution must connect its records to the next action.

## III. How the Sources Fit Together

| Tradition | What it contributes to the argument |
| --- | --- |
| Empiricism, testing and procedural evidence | Encounters through which claims can be constrained; records of how evidence was obtained |
| Social epistemology | Criticism, division of labor, independence and resources as properties of an institution |
| Language and knowledge engineering | A distinction between fluent representation and justification; ways to preserve and use reasons |
| Learning, cybernetics and engineering | Search, feedback, instruments, monitoring and revisable procedures |
| Institutional theory and human choice | Participation, access, dependence and purposes that cannot be supplied by a better factual answer alone |

These traditions supply the inheritance. The book’s proposed addition is to connect them in an executable architecture for agents, then vary the capacity available to that architecture and follow the consequences into human agency. The next question is which of those arrangements work. The afterword names the contributions and separates their proposals from their evidence.
