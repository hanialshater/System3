☕ Coffee & Commute: Call Alberto

Suppose someone tells me penguins live in Italy.

I have never done a census of Italian penguins. I can't check every forest, coastline and piazza. So I call Alberto, who lives in Rome.

"Alberto, do penguins live in Italy?"

He laughs.

I now know more than I did five minutes ago. Not with certainty: Alberto could be wrong, he could have misheard, and an escaped penguin could be crossing Piazza Navona right now, ruining my example.

But look at what came with his answer. He is there. He sees Rome every day. I have a history with him, and if he kept lying about things he's well placed to see, I'd trust him less. If he says "I don't know about all of Italy, but I've never seen one in Rome," the edge of his knowledge is useful too.

Testimony comes with metadata.

Most of what any of us knows arrives this way. I've never measured the speed of light or been to Antarctica. Direct contact doesn't scale, so we lean on people who were positioned to know.

We are all Alberto to someone. People might trust me on ranking systems, because I've spent years on them. If I start confidently explaining whales, that credibility shouldn't come along just because the same mouth is talking.

A language model has read what Alberto wrote. It has no Alberto: no record of who was positioned to know, where a claim came from, how the source behaved before, or where its competence stops. A rumor repeated ten thousand times and a replicated result can arrive in equally polished English.

Making models think harder won't fix that on its own. Reasoning perfectly from a bad premise gets you a beautifully reasoned mistake.

Not every claim needs a dossier. "Alberto said the café is good" is plenty. When the stakes go up, the claim should be able to say who told it.

Who's your Alberto, and on what topics would you stop trusting him?
