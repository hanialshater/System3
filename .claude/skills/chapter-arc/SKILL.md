# How the author builds a chapter

His strongest chapters (4, 5, 7, 8 and 9) share one structure. His weaker chapters (1, 10, 11
and 12) break it in predictable ways. `references/system3-chapter-arcs.md` has the full anatomy
of each chapter, with line references. Read it when you need a worked example of a step.

## The template

1. **Title plus an italic subtitle.** The subtitle is often witty or explanatory.
2. **Open on a concrete thing, never a thesis.** Proven openings:
   - a puzzle the reader takes part in: the camel photo and seven claims, with "The answers are at the end";
   - a joke-line, then a reported case: "Sixteen Claudes walk into a kernel";
   - an ordinary person's small scene: Omar and the dog;
   - a fresh incident: ExploitGym;
   - a callback plus an everyday micro-case: the cathedral, then the cheapest flight.

   Within a page, the author steps in personally. The problem is then posed as a question, or
   loaded into a single word ("better").
3. **One running case, threaded and bookended,** usually with a second, smaller human thread
   (the potter, Omar, Mallorca). Each section returns to the case to show what the new idea
   changes about it. Examples: the store's research agent across Ch7, ExploitGym across Ch8.
4. **Sections are moves, not topics.** There are 7–10 `##` sections, rarely `###`. Their titles
   are vivid phrases, and each section ends on a one-line pivot into the next. The sequence has a
   logic the reader can feel:
   - steps outward (Ch4: body, language, testimony, development…);
   - rungs of a ladder (Ch7);
   - functions of an institution (Ch5);
   - a manager's toolkit in the imperative (Ch8: Retrain Them, Read Their Notes, Make It Ask).
5. **Sources come through scenes, one per point.** A named person, a date and a physical act,
   then turned back onto the case: "The agent version is almost embarrassingly literal."
   Philosophers arrive as tools, not as a survey.
6. **Lived experience at two or three load-bearing points:** an early aside, a mid-chapter
   "this is personal" paragraph, and ideally the author's own experiment or failure. These scenes
   are where the best chapters get their strength. Only the author can supply them.
7. **One coined term,** introduced once, around the middle or later, after the case has earned
   it. Bold it only when it is a definition. Examples: *System 3*, **constitutional surface**,
   *the top loop*, *owning the frontier*.
8. **A turn against the author or the easy reading.** His own experiment loses. The organization
   is the bug. Freezing the evaluator fails. The safety tool removes safety. The same trip
   installs a desire. Put the evidential limits next to the claim.
9. **End with a callback, then an image or a question.** Close the opening case (the camel
   answered, "Sixteen Claudes, Again", Omar's dog), then either reverse the frame ("I usually
   don't know what I want") or ask the question the next chapter answers. No summary.
10. **Length:** 3,000–7,800 words (median about 4,600) and 7–10 sections. Citations can be
    dense, but never arrive as a list.

## The hand-off

Each chapter's last line opens a door that the next chapter's first scene walks through:
- Ch3 ends "How do you know what to trust?" and Ch4 opens "consider a camel".
- Ch10 ends "I spent years building systems that decide what to show her." and Ch11 opens with
  the prototype store.

When planning a chapter, write its last line and the next chapter's first line together.

## How chapters go wrong (diagnose with these)

- **No lived, threaded case.** Ch1 opens on a general riff, and its examples are imagined. Ch12
  hops between topics with no case running through. This is the biggest single gap.
- **Announced coinages.** The terms arrive as bold slogans before anything has earned them (Ch1),
  or several coinages compete (Ch12).
- **Slogan sections.** One-to-three-paragraph sections under consulting-deck headings (Ch10's
  middle).
- **Design-document register.** Many named concepts and "not X, it is Y" closers (Ch11).
- **The turn moved to a coda.** The complication sits in a self-aware ending instead of inside
  the case (Ch11).
- **An imagined lived frame.** "I built a prototype" that never produces a moment the author lived.

## Working method

**Planning a new chapter:**
1. Ask what the chapter must do in the book, using `book-arc` for its position and hand-offs.
2. Choose the opening thing and the running case. Prefer the author's lived experience.
3. List 7–10 sections as moves, each with a one-line purpose and the source or scene that
   carries it.
4. Mark the coined term, the turn and the callback ending.
5. Mark every place that needs the author's own scene as `[AUTHOR: …]`. **Never invent his
   experiences.** Clearly labelled imagined cases ("Imagine…", "Suppose…") are fine. Real people
   and events must come from checkable sources.

**Diagnosing an existing chapter:**
1. Map it onto the template above, as a table with one row per step: present, weak or missing,
   with line references.
2. Name which failure patterns apply.
3. Propose a restructured outline, keeping the author's best passages and noting what moves
   where.
4. Separate the fixes you can make from the material only he can supply.

Do not edit chapter files unless he asks. A diagnosis or outline goes in the reply, or in a
file he names.

## Output

For an outline:

| # | Section title | Move (what it does) | Scene or source | Lived? |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Then give:
- the opening line idea;
- the running case;
- the coined term, and where it lands;
- the turn;
- the ending callback, plus the hand-off to the next chapter;
- the list of `[AUTHOR: …]` needs.

For a diagnosis, use the template table first, then the proposed outline in the same format.
